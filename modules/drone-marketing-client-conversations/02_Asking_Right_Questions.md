# Asking the Right Questions

Most pilots talk too much during sales conversations. The strongest closers do the opposite — they ask careful questions and listen, and the client basically sells themselves. The skill is not charisma; it is having a small set of well-chosen questions ready, and the discipline to wait for the answer.

## Questions Build Trust

Customers cannot tell whether you are a good pilot in five minutes — but they can tell whether you understand their world. A few thoughtful questions about *their* business, *their* timeline, and *their* outcome do more for trust than a portfolio reel. Talking less also signals confidence; pilots who pitch frantically often look unsure of themselves.

> **Important:** Aim for the customer to talk roughly twice as much as you during a discovery call. If you are doing more than 40% of the talking, switch to listening mode.

A fifth category — **decision** — covers who else is involved in the call ("Are you the only person reviewing this, or will the marketing director weigh in?") and what their decision process looks like.

## Outcome Questions That Work

Get specific about the result the customer actually cares about. Useful prompts:

- *"If this goes well, what changes for your business?"*
- *"What would make this a great outcome — and what would make it disappointing?"*
- *"What is the deliverable for? Who else will see it?"*
- *"How will you decide whether the work was successful?"*

These questions feel slightly unusual the first time you ask them. Customers love answering them, because it forces them to think clearly about something they have only half-considered.

## Context Questions That Surface Risk

Past experience is the cheapest source of insight you have:

- *"Have you worked with a drone operator before? How did it go?"*
- *"What is the one thing you do not want to repeat from prior projects?"*
- *"Is anything about this project that gives you pause?"*

The answers reveal where your competitors lost the deal — and where you can differentiate. They also surface red flags. If the prospect's last three vendors all "missed the deadline," ask why. The answer might be vendor failure or might be unrealistic timelines that will burn you next.

> **Tip:** "What did your last drone operator do well that you would want repeated?" is a hidden gem of a question. It tells you exactly what to deliver and how to scope your proposal.

## Logistics Questions That Save You Money

The expensive surprises usually come from logistics. Ask early:

- Site access — gates, keys, NOTAMs, restricted airspace
- Stakeholder availability — does anyone need to be on site?
- Weather flexibility — is the date hard or moveable?
- Power, parking, lighting — anything that affects setup
- Permissions — building owner approvals, insurance certificates needed

A 30-second question on the discovery call can prevent a four-hour drive to a site you cannot fly.

## Budget Questions Without Awkwardness

Budget feels uncomfortable to ask about, but skipping it produces wasted proposals. Two phrasings that work:

- *"To save us both time, do you have a rough budget range in mind for this?"*
- *"Most projects of this scope land between $X and $Y — does that feel reasonable, or were you thinking smaller or larger?"*

The second is especially useful: by anchoring with a real range, you spare the prospect the awkwardness of guessing first.

> **Warning:** "We don't have a budget yet" is rarely true. Usually it means "we have a number we haven't shared." Continue asking gently — *"completely understand. If I sent a $20,000 proposal would that be in the ballpark or way off?"* — until you get a useful range.

## Capturing What You Hear

Take notes during the call (or right after, on a phone call). Write down the customer's *exact words* for the outcome they want — those words go straight into your proposal. Customers respond viscerally to seeing their own language reflected back at them. It signals you listened.

Build a one-page discovery call worksheet with 8–12 of your favourite questions across the five categories. Use it on your next three discovery calls. ## Key Takeaways

- Customers should talk roughly twice as much as you during discovery
- Group questions into outcome, context, logistics, budget, and decision
- Ask explicitly about prior vendors, what worked, and what to avoid — that is where competitive advantage lives
- Anchor budget conversations with a real range to remove awkwardness
- Capture the customer's exact words and reflect them back in your proposal
