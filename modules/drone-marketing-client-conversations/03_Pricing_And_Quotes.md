# Pricing Conversations and Quotes

Pricing is where most drone operators lose money — not by underpricing the work itself, but by mishandling the *conversation* around price. The price you can charge is rarely set by your costs. It is set by how much value the customer perceives, and by how confidently you communicate it.

## Quote Outcomes, Not Hours

Selling time anchors customers to your hourly rate, which they will compare to a hairdresser's. Selling outcomes anchors them to the result, which they will compare to the value of that result. A $1,500 listing reel that helps sell a $1.4M home is a bargain. The same job priced as "12 hours at $125/hour" feels expensive.

> **Important:** Whenever possible, propose a project price tied to a deliverable, not an hourly rate. Hourly pricing punishes efficient, experienced operators and rewards slow ones.

## Anchor First

The first number mentioned in a pricing conversation tends to anchor the entire deal. If the customer names a low number first, you spend the rest of the conversation defending a higher one. If you name a confident number first — supported by the value you uncovered in discovery — you set the frame.

A useful pattern:

> *"Based on what you described — the listing goes live the day after the shoot, and you want a 30-second highlight plus six stills — projects like this typically run between $1,800 and $2,400, depending on whether you want sunrise or sunset coverage. Want me to put together a fixed quote at one of those points?"*

This gives a confident range, ties it to the deliverables, and ends with a question that invites commitment.

Most customers pick the middle option, which is exactly where you want them. The "Good" tier sets a floor and prevents sticker shock. The "Best" tier raises the ceiling and frequently gets picked when the customer's budget is larger than they admitted.

> **Tip:** Tiers should differ in *deliverables*, not just in price. Adding extra deliverables — same-day stills, extra revisions, drone + ground combo — gives the customer real choices and stops the proposal from feeling arbitrary.

## Writing the Quote Itself

A good quote is short, specific, and read in two minutes. The structure that works:

- **Project summary** — one sentence in the customer's own words
- **Deliverables** — bulleted, exactly what they will receive
- **Schedule** — shoot date(s), delivery date
- **Investment** — the price, clearly stated
- **Terms** — payment, cancellation, weather, revisions
- **Acceptance** — a simple way to say yes (e-signature, "reply 'approved'")

Avoid PDFs full of stock photography and 12 pages of fine print. Customers do not read those. They read the first page and the price.

## Talking About Price Out Loud

Saying the price aloud is the moment most operators lose deals. They mumble, soften, or pre-discount. Two simple discipline rules:

- State the price calmly, in a normal voice — never apologetically
- Stay silent for at least three seconds after stating it

The silence is uncomfortable but powerful. The first person to speak after a price is named tends to give ground. Let the customer respond first.

> **Warning:** Avoid pre-discounting before the customer asks. "It's $2,400 — but I could probably do $2,000" trains every future customer to expect a discount and signals you do not believe your own price.

## Handling Price Push-Back

Customers will push on price. That is normal — sometimes a real budget signal, sometimes a habit. A useful first response:

> *"Tell me more — is the price the issue, or is it that the scope feels heavier than what you need?"*

This separates the two situations. If it is scope, drop a deliverable to match a smaller price. If it is genuinely budget, decide whether you can flex without setting a bad precedent. In most cases, hold the price and adjust deliverables instead of cutting the rate.

## When to Walk Away

Not every prospect is a good fit. Walk away when:

- The budget is below your realistic floor
- The customer is openly disrespectful or treats you as commodity labour
- The timeline or expectations are clearly unrealistic
- The work is misaligned with your positioning

A gracious "I do not think I am the right fit, but here is someone who might be" preserves your reputation and frees your calendar for the right work.

Practice saying your price out loud, ten times in front of a mirror, until it sounds calm and unapologetic.

## Key Takeaways

- Quote outcomes and deliverables, not hours
- Anchor pricing with a confident first range tied to discovery findings
- Three-tier proposals (Good / Better / Best) raise average deal size
- A quote is short, specific, and ends with a clear way to say yes
- Say the price calmly and stay silent — never pre-discount
- Walk away from prospects whose budget, behaviour, or scope is fundamentally wrong
