# Handling Objections and Closing

Objections are not failures. They are signals that the customer is engaged enough to push back. The pilots who close more deals are not the ones who avoid objections — they are the ones who treat them as information, respond with calm, and gently bring the conversation to a decision.

## Objections Are Information

When a prospect says "it's expensive" or "I need to think about it," they are giving you data. Each common objection points to one of three things: missing information, an unmet concern, or a pacing problem. Your job is to figure out which.

> **Important:** Treat every objection as a question in disguise. "It's too expensive" usually means "I do not yet see the value." "I need to think about it" usually means "no one has helped me feel confident yet."

In practice:

> Prospect: *"It's a lot more than I expected."*
>
> You: *"That's fair — pricing for aerial work isn't always obvious upfront. Can I ask what you were originally expecting? It will help me see if we are scoping more than you actually need."*

You acknowledged, asked, and now you have permission to either adjust scope or explain value, depending on their answer. No pushing, no defensiveness.

## Common Drone Business Objections — and Real Responses

A short field guide for the objections you will hear most:

- **"It's expensive"** — *"Compared to what?"* If they compare to another quote, ask what was included. If they compare to internal expectation, walk through the value carefully.
- **"I need to think about it"** — *"Of course — what specifically would you like to think through? Sometimes I can help with information you might still need."*
- **"I want to compare quotes"** — *"That's smart. Can I send you a short list of the questions worth asking other operators? Most quotes leave a lot out."* (Which is a soft credibility play.)
- **"My nephew has a drone"** — *"That's great for some shots. The reason businesses hire me is the licensing, the insurance, and the editing — happy to send a quick example so you can compare."*
- **"Can you do it cheaper?"** — *"I could trim the scope to fit a smaller budget. What would feel like fair value to you?"* (Adjust scope, never rate.)

Notice the pattern: questions and reframes, never argument.

## Asking for the Close

Most lost deals are lost not to a competitor but to indecision. The customer never had to say "no" because the operator never explicitly asked for the "yes." Closing is asking, calmly and clearly, for the next step.

A few low-pressure closes that work:

- *"Should I block the date on the calendar while you finalise?"*
- *"If everything in the quote looks right, want me to send the contract over for signature?"*
- *"Are we good to move ahead, or is there one thing still in your way?"*

The last one is gold — it surfaces hidden objections that the customer was not going to mention on their own.

> **Tip:** Always end pricing conversations with an explicit ask. "Let me know your thoughts" is not a close. It is an invitation to never hear back.

## Following Up Without Being Annoying

Most deals require follow-up. The trick is making each follow-up *useful* rather than just a check-in. Bring something to every message — a relevant idea, a similar project you just finished, an article that connects to what they mentioned. A useful follow-up cadence:

- Day 2: confirmation message — "Just making sure my quote landed well in your inbox."
- Day 5: useful nudge — "Was reviewing the quote for you and had an idea about [scope detail]."
- Day 10: gentle close — "Want to leave the proposal open for now or should I close it out?"

After day 10, archive and move on. A "close it out" question is also a respectful way to end without ghosting.

> **Warning:** Avoid follow-up patterns that look automated or desperate — sending five messages in three days, asking "did you get my email?", or bumping the same chain repeatedly without new information.

## After the "Yes"

The moment the customer says yes is the moment your *delivery brand* takes over. Send the contract within an hour. Confirm the schedule in writing. Send a friendly "looking forward to working together" message a day or two before the shoot. Strong post-yes communication is how one-off jobs become repeat clients.

## Key Takeaways

- Objections are information, not rejection
- Use the three-step pattern: acknowledge, ask, reframe
- Never argue — always ask a clarifying question first
- Most lost deals are lost to indecision; close with a calm, explicit ask
- Make follow-ups useful, not nagging — and respectfully close stale conversations
- Strong post-"yes" communication turns wins into repeat clients
