# Understanding Profit and Loss

The Profit and Loss statement (P&L) tells you whether your business is making or losing money. Learning to create and read one is essential.

**Income** is money collected from customers. **Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)** is the direct cost to deliver your product — parts for a manufactured drone, USB drives included with deliveries, etc. For service businesses, COGS is typically minimal. **Gross Profit** equals income minus COGS. **Operating Expenses** are indirect costs regardless of sales volume: marketing, rent, insurance, vehicle costs, software, utilities. **Operating Income** equals gross profit minus operating expenses. **Net Income** is your bottom line after accounting for any other income or expenses.

## Example

| Line Item | Amount |
|-----------|--------|
| Income (drone sold) | $500 |
| Cost of Goods (parts) | -$300 |
| **Gross Profit** | **$200** |
| Operating Expenses | -$150 |
| **Net Income** | **$50** |

## Sales Forecast

Use your market analysis data to forecast sales. How many packages will you sell per month? Be realistic — this is not a wish list. Your forecast becomes your target.

## Chart of Accounts

Organize your finances with a chart of accounts: 4000-series numbers for income, 5000-series for COGS, and 6000-series for operating expenses. This structure feeds directly into your P&L and accounting software.

## Personnel and Taxes

If you have employees, account for salary, payroll taxes, benefits, and PTO. Even as a solo operator, determine what you will pay yourself. Research sales tax requirements in your state — services are typically exempt, but delivering a physical product (like a USB drive) may trigger sales tax.

> **Warning:** Do not underestimate your expenses. It is far better to overestimate costs than to underestimate them. Small expenses add up fast.

## Key Takeaways

- A P&L shows whether you are making or losing money
- Income minus COGS equals gross profit; minus operating expenses equals net income
- Create a realistic sales forecast based on your market analysis
- Never underestimate your costs — account for everything
