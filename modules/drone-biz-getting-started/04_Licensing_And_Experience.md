# Licensing and Building Experience

Before you can legally operate a drone business, you need proper certification. And before you should operate a drone business, you need adequate experience. This lesson covers both requirements.

## The FAA Remote Pilot Certificate (Part 107)

To fly a drone commercially in the United States, you must hold an FAA Remote Pilot Certificate, commonly known as Part 107. This is non-negotiable — the FAA requires it for any drone operation that is not purely recreational.

To obtain your certificate, you must pass a 60-question FAA written exam at an approved testing facility. You need at least 70% correct answers to pass. The exam covers a range of topics including regulations, airspace classification, weather, sectional charts, aeronautical decision-making, airport operations, and radio communication.

> **Important:** Your certificate must be renewed every two years. Even if you are doing free work for a friend's business, you need Part 107 — if the purpose is not purely recreational, commercial rules apply.

## Building Your Experience

If you are fairly new to drones and do not yet have extensive experience, here are proven ways to build your skills before launching a business.

**Work for an existing drone company.** There are established drone businesses that hire pilots. Working for someone else first lets you learn the behind-the-scenes operations, understand client expectations, and develop your skills without the pressure of running your own business.

**Become a contractor.** Companies like DroneUp and DroneBase connect drone pilots with jobs as independent contractors. The pay is not always high, but it is excellent experience. Think of it as seed money for your future business, not beer money.

**Take specialized courses.** Whether it is mapping, inspections, thermography, or photogrammetry — invest in training that is relevant to the niche you want to enter. This course is one piece of the puzzle, but it is not the only training you will need.

**Join a local flying club.** Flying with other pilots, asking questions, and observing different techniques will accelerate your learning dramatically.

**Get industry-specific certifications.** If you want to work in mapping, consider a GIS certification. For inspections, look into relevant professional certifications. These add credibility and open doors that drone skills alone cannot.

**Practice relentlessly.** There is no substitute for flight time. Get comfortable with your equipment in a wide range of conditions before taking on paying clients.

> **Tip:** If you do not feel confident in your ability to execute a specific type of job safely and professionally, you are not ready to charge for it yet. Keep practicing.

## Building Your Portfolio

Even before you start your business, begin assembling a portfolio of your best work. This portfolio will be critical for your website and client meetings. Include a wide variety of settings, lighting conditions, and perspectives. If you do videography, create a demo reel. If you do inspections, document sample projects.

Remember: clients care about results, not equipment. Do not focus your portfolio on showcasing your drone — focus on showcasing the quality of your deliverables.

## Part-Time or Full-Time?

Many successful drone business owners start part-time while keeping their existing job. This is a smart approach that reduces risk. Before going full-time, make sure you have enough savings to weather slow months (drone work is often seasonal and weather-dependent), you have been operating long enough to understand your business cycles, and your revenue is consistent enough to sustain you.

> **Warning:** Do not quit your day job after three great months. Understand the full annual cycle of your business — especially how winter or rainy seasons affect demand in your area — before making the leap.

## Key Takeaways

- Part 107 certification is required for any commercial drone operation in the US
- Build experience through contracting, working for others, and specialized training
- Start assembling your portfolio now — clients want to see results, not equipment specs
- Starting part-time is a smart, low-risk approach
- Have enough savings for slow months before going full-time
